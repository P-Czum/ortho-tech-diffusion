## Title

**Emergence, Origin, and Decline of Surgical Technologies in the Orthopaedic Literature, 2000–2025**

(chosen 2026-09-03; the question-form alternative dropped)

---

## Structured abstract

**Background.** New surgical technologies enter the orthopaedic literature every year, but there is no systematic account of which ones emerged in the 21st century, where research on them was concentrated, or why attention to some of them later faded.

**Questions/purposes.** (1) Which surgical technologies and techniques emerged in the orthopaedic literature between 2000 and 2025, and when? (2) Where was research on each concentrated — in how many countries, and which — and how fast did attention grow? (3) Among technologies whose attention later declined, does the depth of decline correspond to the state of clinical evidence about them?

**Methods.** All PubMed records indexed to MeSH *Orthopedic Procedures* and its descendants, 2000–2025 (⟨N⟩ records after removal of vascular, maxillofacial, veterinary and homonymous literature that the MeSH tree lets in — 9% of records). Noun phrases were extracted from titles and abstracts; a technology was counted as emerging in the first year its share of the field reached five times its 2000–2002 baseline and at least 0.1%, sustained for three years. Category screening and merging of variants yielded 76 technology groups. For each: the effective number of countries, authors and journals contributing (inverse Herfindahl index, country from first-author affiliation); doubling time; peak share; persistence (2025 share as a fraction of peak). For the 13 groups whose attention fell below half of peak, the state of clinical evidence was classified from guidelines, systematic reviews and registries.

**Results.** (1) 76 technologies emerged, from hip resurfacing (2005), computer navigation (2006) and the Ponseti method (2006) to robotic assistance (2020), femoral neck system (2021) and augmented reality (2022); ⟨n⟩ of them date from the first decade and are invisible from a window starting in 2005. (2) Concentration ranged from 1.2 to 14.7 effective countries. Percutaneous kyphoplasty (545 papers, 90.5% from China), percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (81.4% China) and primary hip arthroscopy (89.0% USA) were single-country literatures; cone-beam CT, short-stem hips, volar locking plates and patient-specific instrumentation were spread across 12–15 countries. Fastest growth: hip arthroscopy (doubling 1.7 years), robotic assistance (2.0), AI/machine learning (2.1). The leading country was the United States or China for 61 of 66 technologies; the five exceptions — France (dual-mobility cups), Germany (short stems), Japan (volar locking plates), South Korea (MIPO, open-wedge osteotomy) — are each the technology's country of origin. (3) Twelve technologies lost more than half of peak attention. Measured relative to each technology's own peak, the two whose evidence turned against use (metal-on-metal bearings, hip resurfacing) fell faster and deeper than the other ten in every window — to 0.17–0.25 of peak at 3–5 years versus 0.35–0.73 — but finer gradations of evidence state were not distinguishable. Eleven of 24 apparent declines were changes of name, not of practice.

**Conclusions.** The 21st-century orthopaedic literature shows a small number of technologies with worldwide research communities and a larger number studied almost entirely in one country, most often China or the United States. When attention to a technology fades, the literature registers a negative verdict — a technology rejected on evidence collapses faster and deeper than any other — but it does not register a positive one: a technology adopted into routine care and one whose question remains open decline alike, because both keep generating papers. The literature sees rejection; it does not see acceptance. This rests on 12 cases, two of them rejected, and is stated as an observation.

**Clinical relevance.** A surgeon who finished training in 2005 has since had to learn, evaluate, or decline 76 technologies; this study is the first list of them with dates. It answers questions surgeons actually face. *Is this new?* — the year of emergence tells how long the evidence has had to accumulate. *Is this hype?* — doubling time and peak year tell whether attention is still climbing or has settled. *Will the evidence apply to my patients?* — a technique studied in fourteen countries rests on evidence that has crossed health systems; one studied 90% in a single country may reflect that country's indications, costs, and case mix, and a surgeon elsewhere should ask before adopting. *Nobody publishes on it any more — is it obsolete?* — a collapse in attention marks rejection, and only that: a technology still written about at half its peak may be standard care or an open question, and the count cannot tell which; the kind of study can.

## Section plan

### Introduction (≈ 500 words)
- **P1.** Bibliometric work in orthopaedics has mapped single technologies (the Ponseti method, robotics, 3D printing) or authorship and journals — never the whole set of new technologies entering the field. No one has asked, for orthopaedics as a whole, which technologies emerged in the 21st century, when, and from where. The literature is where emergence can first be seen, because a technique is published before it is taught; this study is the first map of that emergence across the field.
- **P2.** What we can and cannot learn from it: the literature measures attention; attention is not adoption; but attention has a shape — when it starts, how far it spreads, whether it lasts — and the shape may be informative.
- **P3.** Three questions.

### Methods (≈ 1,100 words)
- **Field.** PubMed, MeSH subtree, 2000–2025; four contaminating literatures found and removed (vascular limb salvage, maxillofacial, veterinary, *Traction* homonym; ≈ 9%); rules and PMID lists in Supplement S3. One paragraph.
- **Detecting emergence.** Noun phrases; the baseline-multiple rule; three text variants; why noun phrases and not n-grams, in three sentences, with the pointer to S1 for the preregistered analysis that this replaced.
- **Defining the material.** What counts as a technology (device, material, computational method, operative or periprocedural technique) and what does not (methods, scales, outcomes, diagnoses); screening by an orthopaedic surgeon; merging of abbreviations and variants and why it matters (11 of 24 declines were renamings); 76 groups.
- **Measuring spread and growth.** Effective numbers; country from first-author affiliation, missingness 9%; doubling time; peak; persistence.
- **Classifying evidence state.** Three states, sources, who classified, ⟨blinded re-rating⟩.
- **Preregistration.** One paragraph: registered on OSF; the registered unit and coding scheme did not work (79.8% of the registered core named no technology; human–model κ 0.44 vs 0.70 threshold); all substantive results are exploratory; eleven deviations tabulated in S1.

### Results (≈ 1,300 words; 4 tables, 3 figures)
- **What emerged, when.** Table 1: 76 groups by y₀ with category and peak. Figure 1: timeline 2000–2025, grouped by decade. Text: the first decade (navigation, PEEK, HXLPE, UKA, Ponseti, ACI, locking plates, kyphoplasty); the second (PSI, TXA-era techniques, OLIF, PELD, reverse shoulder); the third (robotics, AI, AR/VR, FNS).
- **Names coined by the arrival of a rival.** Table 4, four rows: the existing standard, the year it acquired a name, the rival, the year the rival became present (first year with ≥ 5 papers — not its emergence year). An illustration of a naming event the detector sees as an emergence; excluded from the material, shown because it is a finding about how the field talks.
- **Where, and how fast.** Table 2: effective countries, top country and its share, doubling time. Figure 2: effective countries vs paper count, labelled extremes. Text: the two poles — single-country literatures (China: kyphoplasty, PELD, OLIF, FNS, PFNA; USA: hip arthroscopy, ATSA, TMR, MUA, EMR) versus worldwide ones (CBCT, short stem, volar plate, PSI, MSC); fastest growers; the caveat that concentration may reflect national publishing habits, stated, not resolved.
- **Who leads.** Own subsection: 61 of 66 led by USA or China; five exceptions, five countries of origin. One paragraph, no table needed — the five names carry it.
- **Why attention fades.** Table 3: 12 declines with persistence at +1–3, +3–5 and +5–7 years after each technology's own peak, evidence state, external support. Figure 3: persistence curves relative to own peak, rejected pair marked. Text: rejected versus not rejected, clean in all three windows; no finer gradation; Ponseti as the case where the literature went quiet because the question closed in favour; navigation absorbed into robotics (0.66 at +3–5); MSC barely falling relative to its own peak (0.73); double-bundle as the technique against which a retronym was coined.

### Discussion (≈ 1,100 words)
- **Summary.** Three findings in three sentences.
- **Two producers and the rest.** Orthopaedic research on new technology is a two-country literature; every other country leads only on what it invented. What this means for where evidence comes from, and for a surgeon in a country that is neither.
- **Single-country technologies.** What it may mean (health-system, training, industry, publishing culture); what would distinguish these; why it matters for a reader outside that country.
- **The asymmetry.** The literature registers negative verdicts and not positive ones — after rejection there is nothing left to study; after acceptance there are registries, series and long-term follow-up, which look in volume like an unresolved dispute's trials and meta-analyses. Distinguishing the two would need study type, not count. This is the paper's central interpretive claim and should be stated once, plainly.
- **Attention and evidence.** Rejected versus not rejected, and why the finer gradient did not survive measurement relative to each technology's own cycle (calendar windows compared technologies 2 and 14 years past peak as if alike — an error the surgeon caught); why n = 2 makes it an observation; the metal-on-metal case as the one with external confirmation.
- **What a practising surgeon does with this.** Reading a technology's position on its curve before adopting it; reading single-country evidence as evidence from one health system; reading silence in the literature as either verdict or victory.
- **How the vocabulary around a technology evolves.** One paragraph, observation only, four numbers already measured: the technology arrives under its full name; the standard it displaces acquires a name of its own (three retronyms, 3–8 years after the rival); its complications acquire names during its rise, before peak (50–75% of pairs, in every band); and finally its abbreviation takes over (abbreviation-only share rising to 33–50%). A life cycle of naming, not of the technology — and none of it predicts the technology's fate.
- **What the literature cannot do.** Complication vocabulary emerges before peak attention for technologies that were later abandoned *and* for those adopted into routine (metal-on-metal and kyphoplasty alike; 50% of pairs in every band). It is a feature of a technology's life cycle, not an early warning — tested and stated so.
- **Limitations — measured, not listed.** Detector sees starts, not gradual growth (PRP never reached 5× baseline); moving the window moves the blind spot (BMP rose before 2000; VR falls out with a longer baseline); attention confounds clinical value with access (ACI vs microfracture); leaving the field looks like fading (MSC); MeSH-defined fields leak, and no systematic test for it exists; MTIX 2022 indexing change real but no measurable effect on ranking at n = 43; the preregistered analysis did not work.
- **Next step.** Confirmatory test of the evidence gradient on another surgical specialty, registered before analysis.
