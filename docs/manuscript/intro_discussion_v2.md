## Introduction

A surgeon who completed training in 2005 has since met, evaluated, adopted, or declined several dozen technologies that did not exist in the literature when training began — robotic assistance, patient-specific instrumentation, endoscopic spine surgery, reverse shoulder arthroplasty, 3D-printed implants, artificial intelligence in imaging. Each entered the literature before it entered the operating room, because a technique is published before it is taught. The literature is therefore the first place where the arrival of a technology can be seen, and the only place where all of them can be seen together.

Bibliometric studies of orthopaedic technology are numerous, but each takes one technology at a time: robotics ⟨ref⟩, artificial intelligence ⟨ref⟩, 3D printing ⟨ref⟩, and others, usually as a count of papers per year with co-authorship and keyword maps. Methods that detect emerging terms across a whole literature exist — most recently temporal n-gram analysis with burst detection and human evaluation ⟨ref: Scientometrics 2026, 10.1007/s11192-026-05744-5 — verify before citing⟩ — and they share our starting point that a rise in a term's use is not the same as an innovation; what they do not do is check, term by term, what the counted words refer to in a clinical field. Taken singly, these studies cannot say whether robotics spread faster than 3D printing, whether a technology led from one country is typical or exceptional, or how many of the "emerging" phrases in a field name a technology at all — because no two of them share a denominator, a detection rule, or a check on what the counted words refer to. There is no map of what entered orthopaedics in the 21st century as a whole.

Two things make such a map harder than it looks. First, a count of papers measures attention in the literature, not use in practice; attention can rise for a technology nobody adopts and fall for one everybody has. Second, the unit counted is a name, and names are not technologies: an old standard acquires a name only when a rival appears, and one technology may carry several names whose fortunes diverge. Any map built by counting must therefore say what it is counting, and must be checked by someone who knows the techniques.

We asked three questions. Which surgical technologies and techniques emerged in the orthopaedic literature between 2000 and 2025, and when? Where did research on each begin, and which country leads it now? And how often does a count of emerging names report something other than an emerging technology?

## Discussion

### Three findings

Forty-eight technologies emerged in the orthopaedic literature in the first quarter of the century, nine of them in the first decade, and more than a quarter are still growing. Research on them is a two-country literature: the United States leads 30, China 13, and the two dominate for different reasons — the United States most often starts a technology and keeps its lead, China most often takes over a technology started elsewhere. And a count of names in the literature misreports technology at both ends of its life: three of the detector's "emerging technologies" were names coined for existing standards when a rival appeared, and ten of twenty-two apparent declines vanished when variant names of one technology were merged.

### Two producers, two mechanisms

Rogers' distinction between invention and diffusion ⟨ref⟩ fits what the country data show, with one adjustment: what we measure is the diffusion of *research attention*, not of clinical adoption, and the two can part company. With that stated, the pattern is clear. Of the 26 technologies led by the country that started them, 23 are American. Of the 14 whose literature moved, 11 moved to China, and in each China's share of that technology's papers exceeded its share of the whole orthopaedic literature in the same years — so the movement is not simply China's growing output. The technologies that moved are characteristic: percutaneous and endoscopic spine surgery, intramedullary and cephalomedullary fixation, regional anaesthesia for arthroplasty — techniques that can be adopted at scale by a large surgical workforce without proprietary platforms. The technologies the United States started and kept are more often platform-bound or registry-driven: robotic assistance, patient-specific instrumentation, hip arthroscopy, reverse shoulder arthroplasty.

The American half of this statement rests on weaker ground than the Chinese. American over-representation among the 23 same-country technologies is modest (median 1.24-fold, below background in six), and the United States produced more orthopaedic papers than any other country in the 2000s, so "the earliest papers are American" is partly a consequence of volume. The Chinese take-overs pass a background control; the American starts do not have one. We state the former as a finding and the latter as an observation.

For a surgeon outside these two countries the practical point is different. Five technologies are led by a third country, and only for one — dual-mobility cups, in France — is that country also where the earliest papers came from. Japan leads volar locking plates, which began in American papers; South Korea leads open-wedge osteotomy and minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis, neither of which it started. Leadership in the literature shows where a technique found its clinical place, which may be far from where it was invented, and the evidence a surgeon reads comes from that place.

### The name is not the technology

The detector in this study counts phrases, and a phrase can emerge for three reasons: a technology is new; an old technology acquires a new name; or an existing name begins to be written where it previously was not. The second and third are not rare. *Single bundle* emerged in 2010 — three years after double-bundle reconstruction appeared — because comparisons needed a name for the technique that had been standard for decades; *anatomic* shoulder arthroplasty and *mechanical* alignment emerged the same way. These events are real and carry information, but it is information about language, and a map that counted them as technologies would be wrong in exactly the cases where competition between techniques is liveliest.

The same confusion operates at decline. Before variant names were merged, 21 names appeared to have lost half their peak attention; after merging, 10 technologies had. Four of the apparent declines were a second name for a technology that was declining anyway — chondrocyte implantation and its abbreviation among them; seven, in four technologies, were names whose usage shifted within a group that, as a whole, did not decline: *kyphoplasty* gave way to *percutaneous kyphoplasty*, the full name of pedicle subtraction osteotomy to its abbreviation, open-wedge high tibial osteotomy to its modified form. Without merging, the paper would have reported twice as many declines as there were, and would have been confident about each.

This is why the registered version of this study did not work. We preregistered an analysis on word n-grams, and 80% of the registered core named no technology at all — fragments of abstract boilerplate and names of study designs. Noun phrases removed the boilerplate, but not the retronyms or the split names; those required a surgeon. We do not think this is a defect specific to our pipeline. Every bibliometric study of a technology counts names, and the single-technology studies in this field validate neither their queries nor what the counted words refer to ⟨ref⟩. The result is a literature that can report a trend for "robotics" without knowing how much of it is *robotic* as adjective, *robot* as noun, and *robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty* as a technique — three series with three different curves.

### What a practising surgeon does with this

Three readings. A technology's year of emergence and doubling time place it on its curve: artificial intelligence and robotic assistance doubled their share in about two years and are still rising; total disc replacement took fifty. The leading country and the effective number of countries say whose evidence a technology rests on: a technique studied across twelve countries has been tried under different indications, costs, and case mix; one studied 80% in a single country has not, and a surgeon elsewhere should ask what in that country's practice made it work. And a technology that dominates the literature or disappears from it may be doing neither — the name may have changed.

### Limitations, as measured properties

Two choices of ours move individual years. The veterinary filter, which removes pre-clinical animal work along with veterinary surgery, delays the emergence of mesenchymal stem cells by four years and of minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis by three, and brings four others forward by one to three years through the smaller denominator; and the second indexing discontinuity in 2024 is visible in descriptor depth, though not in the set of declines. The detector sees starts, not gradual growth: platelet-rich plasma doubled over twenty years and never reached five times its baseline, and is absent. Moving the window moves the blind spot rather than removing it: hip resurfacing is visible only because the window opens in 2000, and bone morphogenetic protein, which rose before 2000 and fell after 2011, is not. Attention confounds clinical value with access: chondrocyte implantation is expensive, regulated as a medicinal product, and performed in few centres, and its attention fell to 0.69 of peak while microfracture, which any arthroscopist performs, held at 0.58, though both remain in use. A topic that leaves the field looks like a topic that fades: mesenchymal stem cells moved into basic science and back. The MeSH-defined field admitted vascular, maxillofacial, veterinary, and homonymous literature amounting to 9% of records; we found these by audit, not by a systematic test, and removed them with rules whose separation we measured. The 2022 change to automated MEDLINE indexing is real — descriptors per record fell from 13 to 9 and recovered — but had no measurable effect on the ranking of our material (ρ = 0.14, p = 0.23, n = 75 phrases). Geographic origin is the country of the earliest papers detected in the window, from first-author affiliation — not the country of invention, which this design cannot establish — and for techniques older than 2000 not even the earliest papers. The veterinary exclusion removes pre-clinical animal studies of human implants and may therefore delay the detected year of emergence for implants and biological therapies; its effect is measured in Supplement S8. Persistence windows that reach beyond 2025 are censored and marked so. The parameters of the detector are choices, and the atlas's sensitivity to them is reported; what cannot be reported is recall — how many technologies that did emerge never reached the candidate list — ⟨because no independent reference list exists / except against the external list in Supplement S9⟩. The candidate screen was performed by one surgeon ⟨; agreement with a second rater on a stratified sample is reported in Supplement S9⟩. Publishing practice changed over the window — the share of records with abstracts rose from 83% to 88% and of English-language records from 90% to 97% — and these trends are part of what the field-share denominator absorbs. And the preregistered analysis did not work; twelve deviations from the registration are tabulated in Supplement S1, with the measurement that forced each and whether the response rule was set in advance (it was, for two).

### Conclusion

The 21st-century orthopaedic literature is a two-country literature in which the United States most often starts a technology and keeps its lead, and China most often takes over a technology started elsewhere. A count of names in that literature misreports technology at both ends of its life — at emergence, when competition gives an old standard a new name, and at decline, when one technology carries several — so bibliometric claims about surgical technologies require review by someone who knows the techniques. The map of 48 technologies, their dates, origins, and leading countries is offered as that review's first product.
