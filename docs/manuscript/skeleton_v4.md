## Structured abstract

**Background.** New surgical technologies enter the orthopaedic literature every year, but there is no map of which ones emerged in the 21st century, where research on them began and where it is now concentrated, or how far the names in the literature can be trusted to stand for technologies.

**Questions/purposes.** (1) Which surgical technologies and techniques emerged in the orthopaedic literature between 2000 and 2025, and when? (2) Where did research on each begin, and which country leads it now? (3) How often does a count of emerging names report something other than an emerging technology?

**Methods.** All PubMed records indexed to MeSH *Orthopedic Procedures* and descendants, 2000–2025 (⟨N⟩ after removal of vascular, maxillofacial, veterinary, and homonymous literature admitted by the MeSH tree, 9% of records). Noun phrases from titles and abstracts; a technology counted as emerging in the first year its share of the field reached five times its 2000–2002 baseline and 0.1%, sustained three years. An orthopaedic surgeon screened candidates and merged variant names, yielding 54 technologies in six kinds. For each: effective number of contributing countries (inverse Herfindahl, first-author affiliation), leading country, country of earliest papers, doubling time, and persistence relative to its own peak.

**Results.** (1) Forty-eight technologies emerged — 9 in 2000–2009, 32 in 2010–2019, 7 since — from hip resurfacing and kyphoplasty (2005) to augmented reality and kinematic alignment (2022); 14 are still growing. Implants diffused slowly and widely (median doubling 18 years, 7.7 effective countries), operative techniques fast and more narrowly (5 years, 5.1 countries). (2) The United States led 30 literatures and China 13. The two dominate for different reasons: of 26 technologies started and led by the same country, 23 are American; of 14 whose literature moved, 11 moved to China, each at 1.5–4 times China's share of the field. Outside these two, a country leads only five technologies, and for only one (dual-mobility cups, France) is it the country of origin. (3) Three "emerging technologies" were names coined for existing standards when a rival appeared (single-bundle ACL reconstruction, anatomic shoulder arthroplasty, mechanical alignment), and 10 of 22 apparent declines vanished when variant names were merged.

**Conclusions.** The 21st-century orthopaedic literature is a two-country literature, with the United States most often starting a technology and keeping its lead, and China most often taking over one started elsewhere. A count of names in the literature misreports technology at both ends of its life — at emergence, when competition gives an old standard a new name, and at decline, when one technology carries several names — so bibliometric claims about surgical technologies require review by someone who knows the techniques.

**Clinical relevance.** A surgeon who finished training in 2005 has since had to learn, evaluate, or decline 48 technologies; this is the first list of them with dates, origins, and the countries whose evidence they rest on. A technique studied across twelve countries rests on evidence that has crossed health systems; one studied 80% in a single country may reflect that country's indications and case mix. And a technology that dominates or disappears from the literature may be doing neither: the name may have changed.

## Section plan

### Introduction (≈ 500 words)
- **P1.** Bibliometric work in orthopaedics has mapped single technologies (the Ponseti method, robotics, 3D printing) or authorship and journals — never the whole set of new technologies entering the field. No one has asked, for orthopaedics as a whole, which technologies emerged in the 21st century, when, and from where. The literature is where emergence can first be seen, because a technique is published before it is taught; this study is the first map of that emergence across the field.
- **P2.** What we can and cannot learn from it: the literature measures attention; attention is not adoption; but attention has a shape — when it starts, how far it spreads, whether it lasts — and the shape may be informative.
- **P3.** Three questions.

### Methods (≈ 1,100 words)
- **Field.** PubMed, MeSH subtree, 2000–2025; four contaminating literatures found and removed (vascular limb salvage, maxillofacial, veterinary, *Traction* homonym; ≈ 9%); rules and PMID lists in Supplement S3. One paragraph.
- **Detecting emergence.** Noun phrases; the baseline-multiple rule; three text variants; why noun phrases and not n-grams, in three sentences, with the pointer to S1 for the preregistered analysis that this replaced.
- **Defining the material.** What counts as a technology (device, material, computational method, operative or periprocedural technique) and what does not (methods, scales, outcomes, diagnoses); screening by an orthopaedic surgeon; merging of abbreviations and variants and why it matters (11 of 24 declines were renamings); 76 groups.
- **Measuring spread and growth.** Effective numbers; country from first-author affiliation, missingness 9%; doubling time; peak; persistence.
- **Classifying evidence state.** Three states, sources, who classified, ⟨blinded re-rating⟩.
- **Preregistration.** One paragraph: registered on OSF; the registered unit and coding scheme did not work (79.8% of the registered core named no technology; human–model κ 0.44 vs 0.70 threshold); all substantive results are exploratory; eleven deviations tabulated in S1.

### Results (≈ 1,500 words; 4 tables, 2 figures) — see `results_v3.md`
- **What emerged, when.** Table 1 (48 rows: y₀, kind, peak, doubling, leading country, country of earliest papers). Figure 1: timeline by kind.
- **Six kinds diffuse differently.** Table 2.
- **Who studied them: start vs. lead.** Figure 2 (started vs leads, by country). 26 same / 14 moved / 8 unclassified; US starts-and-keeps, China takes-over; five exceptions as the special case.
- **Names, not technologies.** Table 3 (three retronyms) + 10 of 22 declines as merge artefacts — equal footing with the atlas.
- **Declines, descriptive.** Table 4, persistence relative to own peak; context column; no evidence thesis.

### Discussion (≈ 1,000 words)
- **Summary.** Three findings in three sentences.
- **Two producers, two mechanisms.** Starting versus taking over; Rogers' distinction between invention and diffusion — applied to *research attention*, not clinical adoption, and stated so. Why China's take-overs are over-represented against background and America's starts are not controlled for volume.
- **The name is not the technology.** Retronyms at emergence; merged names at decline; what the registered n-gram analysis showed (80% of the registered core named no technology). The paper's methodological claim, made once.
- **What a surgeon does with this.** Position on the curve; single-country evidence as one health system's evidence; the name may have changed.
- **Limitations — as measured properties.** Detector sees starts, not gradual growth (PRP); moving the window moves the blind spot (BMP, hip resurfacing, VR); attention confounds value with access (ACI vs microfracture); leaving the field looks like fading (MSC); MeSH-defined fields leak (9%, found by audit); MTIX 2022 real, no measurable effect on ranking (ρ = 0.14, p = 0.18, n = 88); origin measured from 2000, not from invention; the preregistered analysis did not work.
