## What emerged, and when

Fifty-four technologies and techniques emerged in the orthopaedic literature between 2000 and 2025 (Table 1, Figure 1): 11 in the first decade, 36 in the second, and 7 in 2020–2023 — the last figure truncated by design, since emergence requires three confirmatory years.

The earliest were kyphoplasty, vertebroplasty, hip resurfacing, and total disc replacement (all 2005); computer navigation, the Ponseti method, autologous chondrocyte implantation, and locking plates (2006); reverse shoulder arthroplasty, total ankle arthroplasty, and double-bundle ACL reconstruction (2007). The second decade was the decade of percutaneous and endoscopic spine surgery — transforaminal interbody fusion (2010), lateral interbody fusion (2013), endoscopic discectomy (2016), oblique interbody fusion (2018) — together with patient-specific instrumentation (2012), the direct anterior approach (2014), 3D printing (2016), and mesenchymal stem cells (2017). Since 2019: artificial intelligence and robotic assistance (2019), lateral extra-articular tenodesis (2020), the femoral neck system, cephalomedullary nails, and targeted muscle reinnervation (2021), augmented reality and kinematic alignment (2022), ultrasound guidance (2023).

Fifteen of the 54 — more than a quarter — had not peaked by 2025, among them reverse shoulder arthroplasty, robotic assistance, artificial intelligence, 3D printing, patient-specific instrumentation, and kinematic alignment. Doubling times of attention, where computable (44 of 54), ranged from 2.1 years (artificial intelligence) to 57 years (total disc replacement) and are given in Table 1.

## Six kinds of technology diffuse differently

Grouped by kind (Table 2), the 54 technologies show distinct patterns. Implants and materials (10) diffused slowly and widely — median doubling time 18 years, 7.7 effective countries. Operative techniques (23) diffused fast and locally — 5 years, 4.6 countries. Assistive tools — navigation, patient-specific instrumentation, 3D printing, robotics, artificial intelligence, augmented reality, ultrasound guidance — are the youngest group (median emergence 2019) and five of seven are still growing. All three biological therapies declined.

## Who studied them: where a technology starts, and where it ends up

Research on a technology was led — by first-author country, papers from emergence onward — by the United States for 34 technologies and by China for 15; by South Korea for two, and by France, Germany, and Japan for one each. Concentration ranged from 1.5 to 12.8 effective countries. Eight technologies were single-country literatures (effective number ≤ 2.5): four Chinese (percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy, 81% of papers; femoral neck system, 70%; proximal femoral nail antirotation, 69%; oblique lumbar interbody fusion, 65%) and four American (targeted muscle reinnervation, 68%; manipulation under anaesthesia, 67%; labral repair, 64%; hip arthroscopy, 63%). Ten were spread across nine or more effective countries, with no country above 25%. Paper counts did not distinguish the two: endoscopic lumbar discectomy had 320 papers at 1.5 effective countries, volar locking plates 307 at 12.8.

The country that leads a literature is not always the country where it began. For each technology we identified the country of its earliest papers in two ways — the most frequent among the ten earliest, and the most frequent in all papers before emergence — and classified a technology only when both agreed. For 29 technologies the same country started and leads; for 12 the literature moved from one country to another; for 13 the two measures disagreed and no classification was made (Figure 2).

The two dominant countries dominate for different reasons. Of the 29 technologies started and led by the same country, 26 are American. Of the 12 that moved, ten moved to China — from the United States (vertebroplasty, kyphoplasty, transforaminal interbody fusion, pedicle subtraction osteotomy, femoral nerve block, medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction, ultrasound guidance), from South Korea (endoscopic discectomy, femoral neck system), and from Japan (oblique interbody fusion). In every one of these ten, China's share exceeded its share of the orthopaedic literature in the same years (median 2.2-fold, minimum 1.45), so the movement is not an artefact of China's rising output overall. The United States most often starts a technology and keeps leading its literature; China most often takes over a technology started elsewhere and becomes its principal research base. The American half of this statement is weaker than the Chinese: American over-representation among the 26 is modest (median 1.25-fold, below background in six), and the United States was the largest producer of orthopaedic papers in the 2000s, so "the earliest papers are American" is partly a matter of volume.

The five technologies led by neither country are the special case. Only for dual-mobility cups is the leading country also the country of the earliest papers (France, 32.5% of papers, 9.7 times its share of the field). Volar locking plates began in American papers and are led by Japan; open-wedge high tibial osteotomy began in European papers and is led by South Korea. For minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis and short-stem hips the two origin measures disagreed. Leadership in the literature marks where a technique found its clinical place; only sometimes is that where it was invented.

## Names, not technologies: what the detector also found

Three phrases that the detector reported as emerging technologies were names given to existing standards when a rival appeared (Table 3). Single-bundle ACL reconstruction needed no adjective until double-bundle reconstruction appeared in 2007; three years later, comparisons required a name for the old technique, and *single bundle* emerged. Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty was named against reverse arthroplasty, mechanical alignment against kinematic alignment. In each case the only new thing was the rival. These names were excluded from the material and are shown because they mark where a count of phrases measures language rather than technology — and the two part company precisely when competition appears.

The same confusion operates at the other end of a technology's life. Of 22 technologies first identified as declining, 10 disappeared from the list once variants of one name were merged: five were a second name for the same declining technology, and five were names whose fall was a shift of usage within a group that, as a whole, did not decline. Without merging, the paper would have reported twice as many declines as there were.

## Technologies whose attention declined

Eleven technologies lost more than half of their peak attention (Table 4). Because they peaked in different years — computer navigation in 2007, dual-mobility cups in 2020 — persistence was measured relative to each technology's own peak, at 1–3, 3–5, and 5–7 years after it.

The two whose evidence turned against use fell deepest. Metal-on-metal bearings and hip resurfacing retained 0.17–0.26 of peak attention at 3–5 years and 0.11–0.17 at 5–7, against 0.35–0.79 and 0.26–0.72 for the other nine. At 1–3 years the separation is marginal: hip resurfacing retained 0.497 of peak, the Ponseti method 0.500. Among the nine, attention fell for different reasons that the curves alone do not distinguish: the Ponseti method and dual-mobility cups became routine; computer navigation was absorbed into robotic systems and retained 0.68 of peak at 3–5 years; mesenchymal stem cells moved out of the field and back, retaining 0.73; double-bundle reconstruction, total disc replacement, microfracture, and chondrocyte implantation remain open questions.
